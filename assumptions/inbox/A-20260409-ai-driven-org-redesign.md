# Assumption

## Statement

Over the next 3-7 years, AI will cause structural redesign in companies and organizations,
not just incremental efficiency gains. The most effective organizations will redesign work
around AI-native workflows, faster iteration cycles, and selective human involvement at the
highest-leverage steps. In the short term, this will likely increase pressure on humans to
operate at a higher level of judgment and coordination. In the longer term, some of that work
may become standardized, decomposed, and easier to plug into narrower roles.

## Why I Believe This

- AI changes not only the speed of execution, but also who needs to be involved in each step.
- If AI can handle more production and drafting work, organizations will have incentives to
  reduce coordination overhead and keep human involvement focused on direction, quality, and
  exception handling.
- When iteration becomes much faster, existing approval chains and role boundaries may become
  bottlenecks.
- New tools often first increase demands on capable people before processes, infrastructure, and
  role design catch up.

## What Would Make It True

- Companies begin redesigning teams, workflows, and approval processes specifically around AI.
- Smaller teams produce output previously requiring larger cross-functional coordination.
- More roles are defined by judgment, orchestration, verification, and exception handling rather
  than by routine production alone.
- In the short term, high-performing workers are expected to manage broader scopes, faster cycles,
  and more AI-mediated work.
- In the longer term, repeated AI workflows become standardized into narrower operating roles or
  system-managed processes.

## What Would Falsify It

- AI remains mostly an add-on productivity tool, while core company structures stay largely the
  same over the next several years.
- Most output gains come from existing teams using better tools, without major redesign of roles,
  workflows, or approval structures.
- Human involvement does not become more selective or strategically concentrated.
- There is no meaningful short-term increase in the demand for judgment, orchestration, or pace
  from human workers.
- Standardization does not emerge, and work stays equally messy without clear structural changes.

## Minimal Test

- Track 10-15 AI-forward companies for 3-6 months and record evidence of workflow redesign,
  team-size shifts, role changes, and decision-speed changes.
- Collect examples from job descriptions, leadership writing, product shipping patterns, and case
  studies rather than relying only on headlines.
- Separate evidence into three buckets:
  1. AI as efficiency tool only
  2. AI causing workflow redesign
  3. AI causing org redesign
- Revisit whether this assumption is better split into smaller sub-assumptions after the first
  round of evidence.

## Decision Relevance

- If true, career strategy should prioritize leverage, judgment, systems thinking, and human-in-
  the-loop design over routine execution alone.
- If true, personal working methods should adapt to faster iteration and more AI-native workflows.
- If true, evaluating companies should include how well they redesign around AI, not only whether
  they use AI tools.
- If false, a more conventional skill-building and organizational strategy may still be sufficient.

## Notes

- This is currently a compound assumption and may later be split into:
- AI changes org structure more than typical tooling shifts.
- AI raises the short-term bar for human workers.
- AI eventually standardizes some higher-pressure work into narrower repeatable roles.
- The useful question is not whether AI helps, but whether it changes the design logic of work.
